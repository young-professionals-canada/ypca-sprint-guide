## Start with the visitor, not the decoration

Your site may be visited by recruiters, hiring managers, clients, collaborators, mentors, or community partners. Choose one as the primary audience. The website can serve other visitors, but one priority creates clearer decisions.

A useful positioning statement combines your current identity, area of focus, and direction. For example: “Public policy graduate using research and data visualization to make city services easier to understand.”

Avoid empty labels such as “passionate professional,” “visionary,” “results-driven,” and “innovative thinker.” Show those qualities through specific work.

## Select a structure that matches your evidence

Most sites need navigation, a focused hero, a short About section, selected work, experience or education, and contact information. Adapt the labels to your field:

- Technology: Projects and Technologies
- Humanities: Research and Writing
- Business: Experience and Case Studies
- Design: Selected Work and Process
- Science: Research and Field Experience

Do not create Projects, Testimonials, Publications, or Awards sections when you have no truthful content for them.

## Choose a visual system, not a costume

A visual direction should improve hierarchy and confidence without turning your career into a stereotype. Decide how headings feel, how wide paragraphs should be, how work examples are grouped, and how much contrast or colour is appropriate.

The existing course examples offer four starting points: Classic Editorial, Futuristic Analytical, Retro Humanist, and Clean Minimal. Use them as design systems—not as permission to add fake charts, decorative clichés, or unsupported metrics.
