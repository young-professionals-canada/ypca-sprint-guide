## Responsive does not mean “smaller desktop”

On a narrow screen, content must rearrange. Multi-column sections may stack, navigation may collapse, headings may wrap, and buttons may need more room. Test the same content at several widths because a layout can look excellent on a laptop and fail completely on a phone.

Look for text that becomes too small, clipped headings, horizontal scrolling, overlapping controls, tiny touch targets, and important information pushed far below decorative content.

## Accessibility improves the experience for everyone

A basic review should confirm:

- text and controls have enough contrast;
- headings describe the page in a logical order;
- links make sense without surrounding text;
- keyboard focus is clearly visible;
- every meaningful image has useful alternative text;
- motion is subtle and does not block reading;
- buttons and links work without precise mouse movement.

Do not use colour alone to communicate status. Do not hide essential information inside hover effects that phone and keyboard users cannot access.

## Test the message, not only the mechanics

Ask a reviewer to look at the top of the page briefly, then answer: Who is this person? What field or opportunity are they focused on? What should I click next? Their answers reveal whether the hierarchy works.

> A website is ready when it is accurate, understandable, usable, and complete enough to support your current goal—not when every possible feature has been added.
