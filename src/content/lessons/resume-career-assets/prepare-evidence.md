## Start with facts, not polished sentences

A strong resume is not created by finding impressive words. It is created by selecting accurate evidence and explaining why it matters. Before writing, collect the raw material that proves what you did.

Evidence can include schedules you managed, tools you used, presentations you delivered, projects you completed, people you supported, problems you solved, and results you can verify. Coursework, volunteering, family responsibilities, clubs, and independent projects can all count when they demonstrate relevant skills.

> **Truth rule:** If you cannot verify a number, date, tool, responsibility, or outcome, leave it out for now or place it on your follow-up list. Never ask AI to fill the gap.

## Use the evidence test

For each experience, answer four questions:

1. **Context:** Where were you, and what was the goal?
2. **Action:** What did you personally do?
3. **Tools:** What methods, software, equipment, or knowledge did you use?
4. **Proof:** What changed, improved, finished, or became possible?

You do not need a dramatic achievement for every experience. Reliable contributions—organizing information, supporting customers, coordinating people, improving clarity, or completing work accurately—are useful when described specifically.

## Example: turn a vague note into evidence

“Helped with events” is too vague. A more useful source note is: “Coordinated volunteer sign-in for three community events, prepared attendance sheets in Excel, answered participant questions, and reported attendance totals to the program lead.”

The second version is not yet a resume bullet. It is evidence that can later support several truthful versions.
