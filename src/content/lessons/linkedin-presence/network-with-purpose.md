## A network is a set of relationships, not a score

Start with people you already know: classmates, colleagues, managers, instructors, volunteers, event participants, community leaders, and clients when appropriate. Then move outward to alumni, speakers, professionals in relevant teams, and people doing work you genuinely want to understand.

LinkedIn describes connecting as most effective when there is a clear professional reason, such as having worked or studied together, meeting at an event, or wanting to continue a conversation.

## Establish context before asking

Before sending an invitation to someone new, read their profile and recent public activity. Learn enough to explain why the connection makes sense. A strong note contains:

1. a real point of context;
2. one specific reason their work is relevant;
3. a low-pressure invitation to connect.

Free-account limits on personalized invitation notes can change, so use notes for connections where context matters most. Do not automate invitations or send large batches of identical text. LinkedIn may restrict invitation activity reported as unwanted or spam.

## Follow up like a professional peer

After someone accepts, do not immediately demand time, a referral, or hidden job information. Thank them when appropriate, continue the relevant conversation, share a useful resource, or ask one focused question that cannot be answered through basic research.

Relationships become stronger through multiple useful interactions over time. Keep simple notes outside LinkedIn about how you met, shared interests, and any promised follow-up—without collecting sensitive personal information.

> A good connection request makes the relationship clearer. A good follow-up gives the relationship somewhere respectful to go.
